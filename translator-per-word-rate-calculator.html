How to work out your per-word rate as a freelance translator

The calculator assumes your rate covers three things: what you want to earn, your business expenses and your self-employed social security contribution. It does the sum with the 2026 contribution table, and the sections below explain where each number comes from. It is for freelance translators with their tax residence in Spain, registered in the self-employed scheme (RETA) and taxed under direct assessment. It is not tax advice.

The rate it gives is excluding VAT and rounded up. The calculator does not include income tax, takes what you want to earn as net income to find the bracket, assumes you contribute on the minimum base of your bracket and does not apply the reduced contribution for people starting out (section 2).

1. What your rate has to cover

  • What you want to earn a year, after your expenses and self-employed contribution, and before income tax.
  • Your business expenses: licences, equipment, accountant, training… The deductible expenses guide explains what can be deducted, and how.
  • Your self-employed contribution, which depends on your income. The calculator looks it up in the 2026 table.

What you need to invoice a year is the sum of the three. Divided by the hours you bill, it gives the rate per hour; divided by the words you translate in that time, the rate per word.

2. The self-employed contribution in 2026

How the bracket is chosen. Self-employed people contribute according to the annual income obtained from their economic activities (General Social Security Act, art. 308.1). During the year, you choose your monthly contribution base according to your forecast of the monthly average of your annual net income (art. 308.1.a, rule 1). For the adjustment, when the business is under direct assessment, the computable income is net income increased by the owner's social security contributions; a 7% deduction for generic expenses is applied to it, and the resulting amount is spread proportionally over the period being adjusted (art. 308.1.c, rules 1 to 3).

The 2026 table. In the adjustment, the bracket is not looked up with plain net income but with the computable income from the previous paragraph: net income plus the contribution, with the 7% deduction. Each bracket of monthly net income has a minimum and a maximum base (Order PJC/297/2026, art. 18.1), and you can opt for any base in the tables (General Social Security Act, art. 308.1.a, rule 3). The monthly contribution is obtained by applying the contribution rates to the base (art. 308.1.b). The last column is the result of applying 31.5% to the minimum base and rounding to the cent; if each item is rounded separately, three brackets change by one cent (minimum bases of €849.67, €1,143.79 and €1,356.21):

Where the 31.5% comes from. It is the sum of the 2026 rates: 28.30% for common contingencies, 1.30% for occupational contingencies and 0.90% for the intergenerational equity mechanism (Order PJC/297/2026, art. 18.2), plus 0.90% for cessation of activity and 0.10% for vocational training (art. 37, sections 5 and 6). The contribution in the table is that percentage applied to the minimum base, rounded to the cent.

The base is provisional. The bases chosen during the year are provisional until they are adjusted (General Social Security Act, art. 308.1.a, rule 6), which is done on the basis of the annual income reported by the tax authorities from the following year onwards (art. 308.1.c). If what you contributed is below the contribution on the minimum base of the bracket your income falls into, you pay the difference; if it is above the contribution on that bracket's maximum base, the General Treasury of Social Security refunds the difference (art. 308.1.c, rule 4).

If you are starting out. Someone registering for the first time, or who has not been registered in the two years immediately before (three, if they had these reductions in their previous period of registration), pays a reduced contribution for common and occupational contingencies for the twelve full calendar months after registering, without contributing for cessation of activity or vocational training. After that, another reduced contribution may apply for twelve more full calendar months to someone whose annual net income is below the annual minimum wage. Both must be requested: the first when registering; the second before that period starts, with a statement that the expected income will be below the minimum wage (Self-Employment Statute, art. 38 ter, sections 1 to 4). The annual amount of the reduced contribution is set in the relevant State Budget Act; this guide does not give it and the calculator does not apply it.

3. The hours and the words, yours

  • Billable hours are the ones that end up on an invoice, and they are not all your working hours: finding clients, keeping your accounts or training also take time. It is worth entering only the billable ones.
  • Working weeks: take off holidays, public holidays and a margin for sick leave or weeks without work.
  • Words per hour: the closer the figure is to your real pace, the more reliable the rate. If you track the hours on each job, divide the words by the hours of a few similar jobs.

4. If your jobs come with a CAT analysis

When an agency applies a match discount grid, it does not pay you for the words in the text, but for the weighted words. In the example in the CAT analysis rates guide, 5,000 words come to 3,405 weighted words: 68.1% is paid. If you enter that percentage in the last field, the calculator tells you which per-word rate you would need to set to keep covering the same.

What it does not calculate

  • Income tax: what you want to earn is before that tax.
  • VAT: the rate is excluding VAT.
  • The reduced contribution for people starting out.
  • A contribution base other than the minimum of the bracket.
  • Under simplified direct assessment, net income is calculated by also deducting provisions and hard-to-justify expenses: generally 5%, up to €2,000 a year (IRPF Regulation, art. 30.2.ª; for activities in Ceuta, the IRPF Act sets a different percentage for 2026, sixty-fourth additional provision). The calculator does not deduct it, so if you use that method it may place you in a higher bracket than yours.
  • Pluriactividad (contributing at the same time for employment with someone else), the self-employed person's relatives included in the scheme, and company partners or directors, which have their own rules (General Social Security Act, arts. 305.2, 308.1 and 313).
